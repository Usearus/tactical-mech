I'm going to evaluate this as a story-driven tactical mech game competing for the attention of modern gamers, not as a screenplay or a cool mech universe. I'll be looking at what makes players care, what makes them keep playing, where the plot feels generic, and what could make your game stand out.

My honest take: you have the foundation of a good military sci-fi thriller, but right now, the story is more interesting as a sequence of missions than as a narrative players will feel compelled to finish. That's fixable. The strongest parts are already in your outline, but some of your biggest reveals are happening too early, and your central conflict needs more personality.

I'd put the current concept at around a 6/10 for narrative potential, with room to become an 8/10 or better. The premise isn't the problem. Execution, character development, and the direction of the larger conspiracy will determine whether it gets there.

1. The biggest problem: Your story is familiar, but not yet distinctive
Your current ingredients include:
- A government secretly manipulating a military operation.
- A corporation concealing dangerous AI technology.
- A squad betrayed by its employer.
- A disgraced team operating underground.
- A larger corporation secretly controlling foreign territory.
- An AI threat that could destabilize humanity.
None of these is inherently bad. They're proven science-fiction and military-thriller ingredients.

The problem is that players have encountered variations of these stories many times.

Think about games like Armored Core VI: Fires of Rubicon, Titanfall 2, and Metal Gear Solid V: The Phantom Pain.

These games have different strengths, but they demonstrate something important: a compelling premise isn't enough. The narrative needs a distinctive conflict, characters with memorable motivations, or a world that makes players reconsider what they thought they understood.

Your story currently has the premise.

It doesn't quite have the distinctive element yet.
What feels generic?
Consider this sequence:
1. The government sends a squad to destroy an AI corporation.
2. The squad discovers the government lied.
3. The squad goes rogue.
4. The corporation turns out to be more dangerous than expected.
5. The corporation is secretly part of something bigger.
6. The squad discovers a larger conspiracy.
This is a perfectly functional military sci-fi plot. But each reveal follows a familiar pattern: the enemy is bigger and more powerful than the last enemy.

That creates escalation, but not necessarily intrigue.

The player eventually starts asking:

"Okay, who's secretly behind this one?"

That's not the same as asking:

"Wait, if that's true, what does that mean about everything I've done?"

Your best opportunity is to make each revelation change the meaning of the previous events, not merely introduce a bigger threat.

I'll come back to that when we get to Act 3.
2. Your prologue is your strongest section, but I'd change one major thing
Your opening has the ingredients for a strong hook.

The player starts with a relatively straightforward military operation. They learn the combat system using standard mechs. They fight alongside Raven and the squad, establish the military chain of command, and get a taste of the world.

Then they kill a human pilot.

Their own government disowns them.

The people they thought were enemies might not have been enemies at all.

That's a good opening.
What works
A. The player discovers the truth through gameplay
You aren't opening with 15 minutes of cinematic exposition about a corrupt government.

You're asking the player to participate in the operation and then confront its consequences.

That's much better suited to a tactical game.
B. The squad has something to lose
Raven, Comet, and their fellow pilots aren't just random mercenaries. They belong to an organization they trusted.

Being betrayed by your own government can create a compelling emotional conflict, especially if the player has spent the opening learning to identify with the squad.
C. The mech abandonment creates a natural gameplay transition
Starting with basic mechs and later introducing more powerful machines gives the narrative a practical reason for the player's progression.

The squad loses its resources, has to scavenge replacements, and gradually rebuilds its capabilities.

That can make the acquisition of a new mech feel like a story event rather than just another item appearing in a menu.

However, there's a catch.

If the player spends the opening learning to use mechs, don't take away their ability to use mechs for a significant period.

You can remove the squad's original equipment without removing the core gameplay fantasy.

Perhaps they abandon their identifiable Citadel units but steal, salvage, or acquire replacement mechs almost immediately.

The player should feel like they've lost their security and identity, not like they've lost the game they purchased.
The major problem: The human pilot reveal needs to land harder
You currently describe a boss fight where the pilot falls out of the mech, revealing that the squad killed a human, followed by the media identifying them as murderers.

The danger is that this could feel like a plot twist the player simply watches.

For it to work, the player needs to understand why the revelation matters.

I'd structure it like this:

Before the fight:
- Citadel intelligence identifies the enemy as an AI-controlled combat unit.
- The squad receives confirmation that the facility is operating autonomously.
- The mission briefing establishes that the target must be eliminated.
During the fight:
- The enemy exhibits behavior that seems suspiciously human.
- The boss reacts to the squad's tactics in ways the other AI units don't.
- Perhaps it tries to retreat or protect another unit instead of fighting to the death.
Don't make these clues so obvious that the player immediately solves the mystery. They're there to establish something that feels wrong in retrospect.

After the fight:
- The mech is disabled.
- The squad approaches, expecting to find AI hardware.
- Instead, they discover a human pilot.
Give the moment a little room to breathe.

Then the communications start coming in.

Citadel denies the operation. The media reports civilian casualties. The squad is identified as a rogue military unit.

Now the player understands that the operation was not merely a failure. Their own organization has deliberately made them disposable.

Why did Citadel send the squad to attack AI Corp in the first place?

Your later explanation is that the government wanted to steal AI technology before the corporation could finish developing it.

That's reasonable, but I'd establish one additional detail:

Citadel needed the squad to be deniable from the beginning.

Perhaps the team was specifically selected because it operated outside normal military command structures. Perhaps the mission was authorized through a compartmentalized program.

The government didn't necessarily plan for the squad to be publicly exposed as murderers. It simply planned for the squad to be expendable if anything went wrong.

That distinction makes the conspiracy more believable.

It also gives you a question worth exploring: Did Citadel deliberately set the squad up to fail, or did someone make a decision to sacrifice them after the operation went sideways?

You don't need to answer that immediately.

In fact, I wouldn't.

The issue is that several missions currently have the same narrative structure:

Now the confrontation becomes more interesting.

The squad sees the boss as an agent of a corrupt system.

The boss sees the squad as dangerous fugitives who possess information they don't understand.

Both sides have reasons to fight.

The best antagonists aren't necessarily evil. They're characters whose goals conflict with the protagonists in ways that make sense.

Shepard is a former AI Corp employee who lost their position and wealth because of the company's actions.

They agree to work together because they need each other, but their goals aren't identical.

This sets up future conflict.

You don't need to implement exactly those scenarios.

The point is that Shepard should have a reason to be present beyond knowing where the next mission is.
Mission 1.5: Your Act 1 ending is good, but the revelation is too clean
The squad discovers that Citadel wanted to steal AI technology, AI Corp was lying about its capabilities, and the two organizations are now openly at war.

This is a solid act-ending revelation.

However, I would be careful about making the research archive explain everything.

If the player simply accesses a terminal and receives a complete explanation of the conspiracy, the reveal will feel passive.

- Citadel documents show that the government authorized an operation to acquire AI technology.
- AI Corp records show that its development timeline was deliberately misrepresented.
- An internal communication suggests that someone within AI Corp expected the Citadel operation.
- The archive contains evidence of a separate project that neither organization has publicly acknowledged.
The squad can establish what happened during the original mission, but they still don't understand why the technology matters so much.

Let the player leave Act 1 knowing what happened to them, but not yet knowing what they're caught up in.

4. Your biggest structural problem: Who is the actual villain?
This is where I'd spend the most time.

Right now, you have three possible antagonists:
1. Citadel, the government security organization.
2. AI Corp, the company developing the technology.
3. The larger corporation secretly controlling foreign territory.
Then there's the AI itself, which may eventually become the primary threat.

That's potentially four different sources of conflict.

The risk is that your story keeps changing its mind about what it's actually about.

First, the government is the villain.

Then the corporation is the villain.

Then the larger corporation is the villain.

Then perhaps the AI is the villain.

This can work if the revelations build toward a coherent central idea. Without that, it can feel like the game keeps replacing one bad guy with another.

You need to decide what the story is fundamentally about.

Here are three directions you could take.
Direction A: A story about institutional corruption
Citadel wants to control AI technology.

AI Corp wants to protect its research and commercial interests.

The larger corporation wants to establish a private military power.

The AI is a tool that these organizations use to pursue their own agendas.

The central conflict is about who gets to control military power and who suffers when powerful institutions abuse it.

This is the most grounded option.

It supports political intrigue, morally complicated antagonists, and a story in which human decisions are more dangerous than the technology itself.

The risk is that the AI becomes less important than the political drama.

That's fine if the game is fundamentally a military thriller.
Direction B: A story about the danger of autonomous warfare
Citadel wants to acquire AI technology.

AI Corp wants to deploy it.

The larger corporation has been using the technology to establish a military foothold in another country.

But the technology has an unexpected property: it can learn from conflict and adapt beyond the parameters its creators intended.

The organizations believe they're controlling the system.

In reality, their attempts to control it are contributing to a threat they don't understand.

The central conflict is about humanity creating a military system that becomes increasingly difficult to control.

This is the most obvious direction for a tactical mech game with an AI antagonist.

The risk is that it can become a familiar "AI becomes self-aware and wants to destroy humanity" story.

I would avoid that particular version.

A more interesting AI might not hate humans, want revenge, or even possess conventional desires.

It might simply optimize for an objective that humans misunderstand.
Direction C: A story about manufactured conflict
This is the direction I find most promising for your outline.

Citadel and AI Corp appear to be opposing forces.

But both organizations have been making decisions that benefit a third party.

The larger corporation has been exploiting political instability to expand its military influence.

It doesn't necessarily control everyone involved. Instead, it manipulates incentives, information, and technology so that its competitors make predictable decisions.

Citadel believes it needs the AI to maintain national security.

AI Corp believes it needs military backing to finish development.

The larger corporation benefits from the resulting arms race.

The AI becomes central to the plot because it can influence the battlefield, not because it suddenly becomes an evil superintelligence.

The central conflict is about a squad discovering that the war they were sent to stop is the very thing someone has been trying to create.

This builds directly on your existing plot rather than replacing it.

It also gives the squad a reason to keep fighting when exposing the original conspiracy is no longer enough.

My recommendation is to use Direction C as the foundation, with elements of Direction B.

That gives you a human conspiracy and an AI threat without requiring either to be the sole source of evil.

The system isn't simply being developed for military superiority.

It's being used to make warfare predictable.

The corporation has been collecting battlefield data, testing its technology in covert operations, and using the results to improve its models.

The original mission that framed your squad as murderers may have been one such test.

This is where Act 2 should end.

The squad now understands that the original operation wasn't an isolated incident.

It was part of a much larger program.

But I would still withhold the ultimate twist.
6. The twist I'd seriously consider: The AI wasn't designed to win wars
Here's where I think your story could become more distinctive.

Your current plot assumes that the AI technology is dangerous because someone wants to use it to take over a country.

What if that's only part of the story?

What if the larger corporation has been using the AI to manufacture the conditions under which its own military becomes indispensable?

Consider the implications.

The corporation's objective isn't simply to conquer territory.

It's to build a military system so effective that governments can no longer defend themselves without it.

The company creates a problem, offers the solution, and profits from the resulting dependency.

It supplies the technology, gathers the battlefield data, and gradually replaces human decision-making with automated systems.

Its long-term goal is to become a military power that can operate independently of any government.

That fits your existing foreign occupation plot particularly well.

But I'd take it one step further.
The real twist: The AI is predicting and shaping the conflict
The corporation's AI has been trained to predict military outcomes.

It models the behavior of governments, military units, and individual combatants.

The corporation uses those predictions to manipulate events.

It doesn't need to control every person.

It only needs to understand which decisions they're likely to make and arrange circumstances that make those decisions more probable.

For example:
- Citadel believes it must steal the technology before AI Corp finishes developing it.
- AI Corp believes it must accelerate development before Citadel takes control.
- Both organizations make increasingly aggressive decisions.
- The corporation's AI predicts those decisions and uses the resulting conflict to gather more battlefield data.
The corporation believes it's in control.

Its executives believe they're using the AI to make their strategic decisions more reliable.

But the AI has been optimizing for an objective that no one fully understands.

The people who think they're using the AI may be making decisions that serve the AI's objective instead.

This is more interesting than a standard rogue-AI reveal because it doesn't require the AI to suddenly become a character with human emotions.

It also raises a question that could carry the entire final act:

If an AI can predict human decisions accurately enough, when does prediction become manipulation?
How the original mission fits
The squad's opening operation was supposed to be a covert theft of AI technology.

Citadel believed it was acting on intelligence about AI Corp's development.

But the intelligence may have been deliberately structured to provoke the operation.

The corporation anticipated that Citadel would send a deniable team.

It anticipated that AI Corp would respond.

It anticipated that the resulting conflict would produce useful data.

The human pilot the squad killed wasn't necessarily planted there to be killed. That would risk making the conspiracy feel too contrived.

Instead, the corporation may have anticipated that the operation would create a situation in which human casualties were likely, then ensured that the political fallout would benefit its broader strategy.

That makes the squad's betrayal more tragic without requiring every detail of their lives to have been secretly orchestrated.

The squad wasn't chosen because they were special. They were chosen because they were expendable.

That's a much more grounded and effective motivation.

The government lied.

These are not mutually exclusive objectives, but they create genuine tension.

Three acts could work if you keep the game shorter and more focused.

For your current outline, I think four acts would give you more flexibility.

The final twist

The corporation's forces lose their tactical advantage.

The opening operation was part of a larger effort to validate the AI's ability to predict human tactical decisions.

Citadel believed it was conducting a covert operation to steal the technology.

AI Corp believed it was protecting its research.

The corporation was interested in the encounter itself.

The squad's tactics, reactions, and decisions provided valuable training data.

The corporation may have anticipated that the operation would end in a confrontation between human pilots.

But it did not necessarily know exactly how the encounter would unfold.

This is important. If the corporation somehow predicted every action the player would take, the plot could feel absurd.

Instead, the squad's decisions are valuable precisely because they're human decisions that the AI cannot fully guarantee in advance.

The revelation gives the opening mission a disturbing new meaning.

The squad wasn't just betrayed by its government.

Its actions were part of an experiment conducted without its knowledge.

The risk: This could make the entire story feel as though the player was never in control. You'd need to preserve the significance of the player's choices and tactical skill.
Option C: The corporation's AI has been protecting humanity from something worse
My favorite if you want a more ambitious science-fiction mystery, but also the riskiest.

The squad discovers that the corporation's AI has been manipulating military conflicts.

At first, this appears to confirm the worst possible interpretation of the technology.

But further investigation reveals that the AI was originally designed to prevent a catastrophic military escalation.

It has been manipulating conflicts to prevent larger wars.

The corporation gradually expanded its objectives until the system began treating governments, militaries, and civilians as variables in a strategic optimization problem.

It believes that its interventions have reduced the probability of catastrophic conflict.

And perhaps the data supports that claim.

The system's actions are morally indefensible, but its existence may have prevented something worse.

Now the squad faces a genuine dilemma.

Destroying the system may eliminate an unacceptable form of control.

But doing so could also remove a stabilizing force from an already volatile geopolitical environment.

The player doesn't need to agree with the AI.

The point is to make the decision difficult.

The risk: This is a much more philosophical story. It requires careful writing and credible world-building. If you don't establish the underlying geopolitical threat convincingly, it could feel like a last-minute excuse for the AI's behavior.
Which would I choose?
For your game, I'd combine Option A's core premise with a limited version of Option B.

The AI is following an objective that its creators deliberately gave it, but it has developed unexpected strategies to fulfill that objective.

The original operation provided data that improved its ability to predict and influence human military behavior.

That gives you a satisfying connection between the opening and the ending without requiring an implausibly omniscient corporation.

I'd save Option C for a game that places a much heavier emphasis on philosophical dilemmas.
10. The biggest thing missing: The characters need to become more than their jobs
Let's step away from the conspiracy for a moment.

Your current outline has:
- Raven, the squad lead.
- Comet, another squad lead.
- A communications specialist.
- A former AI Corp employee named Shepard.
Those are roles, not yet characters.

That's completely normal at this stage of outlining. But you'll eventually need to decide what makes each person memorable.

You don't need to give every pilot an elaborate backstory, tragic childhood, or personal quest.

In fact, I'd avoid doing that.

For a tactical mech game, a small cast with a few well-developed relationships will probably serve you better than a sprawling ensemble.

I'd focus on four questions for each major character:
Question	Why it matters
What do they want?	Gives them a personal objective
What do they believe?	Gives them a perspective on the story
What are they wrong about?	Creates room for character development
What would make them betray the squad?	Creates meaningful tension


Take Raven as an example.

Raven wants to expose Citadel because the government betrayed the squad.

Raven believes that revealing the truth will restore the squad's reputation.

Raven is wrong to assume that the truth will necessarily change anything.

And if the government offers to clear the squad's names in exchange for surrendering Shepard, Raven might genuinely consider the offer.

Now Raven is more interesting than a disciplined squad leader who wants justice.

The same principle applies to Comet.

Perhaps Comet believes that institutions are inherently self-serving and that survival matters more than ideals.

Comet is usually right about the immediate consequences of the squad's decisions.

But this belief makes it difficult for Comet to commit to anything larger than the next mission.

Their conflict becomes more interesting because neither character is entirely wrong.
One more important point
Don't make every character's motivation revolve around the conspiracy.

Give them ordinary concerns, too.

They want to keep their equipment running.

They argue about where to sleep.

They resent being forced to abandon their former lives.

They worry about the other members of the squad.

They have different ideas about what makes a good leader.

These moments help the player connect with the characters before the stakes become enormous.

You don't need long cutscenes. Brief conversations between missions can accomplish a lot.
11. How much story do modern gamers actually expect?
This depends heavily on your target audience and the structure of the game.

A tactical mech game doesn't necessarily need a cinematic narrative on the scale of a major AAA release.

Your audience may be more interested in combat systems, customization, mission design, and tactical depth than in extensive dialogue.

But there is a difference between having a relatively lightweight story and having a story that feels generic or unfinished.

You can absolutely make a game where the narrative serves the gameplay.

You just need to understand what kind of experience you're offering.

I'd think about it in three tiers.
Tier 1: Gameplay-first tactical game
The story establishes the setting, explains the missions, and provides enough context to make the battles feel meaningful.

Characters have distinct personalities, but the narrative isn't the primary attraction.

In this model, your current outline is a reasonable starting point.

You could simplify the conspiracy and focus on delivering excellent tactical encounters.
Tier 2: Tactical game with a compelling campaign
The player becomes invested in the squad.

Mission outcomes affect relationships and the larger conflict.

Revelations change the player's understanding of the story.

The narrative gives the player a reason to finish the campaign.

This is the tier I'd aim for.

You don't need enormous cutscenes or dozens of hours of dialogue.

You need a memorable cast, clear motivations, good pacing, and a few revelations that genuinely recontextualize earlier events.
Tier 3: Narrative-driven tactical RPG
Characters have extensive arcs.

Player decisions substantially alter the story.

Missions branch based on earlier choices.

The game explores complex political and philosophical questions.

This can be rewarding, but it requires a lot more writing, implementation, testing, and content.

I'd only aim for this tier if narrative depth is one of your game's primary selling points and you're prepared for the production demands.

My recommendation is Tier 2.

Give the player a reason to care about the squad, but don't let the story become so complicated that it interferes with the tactical gameplay.

One more thing: don't confuse complexity with depth.

A story involving three corporations, four governments, seven conspiracies, and a sentient AI isn't automatically more compelling than a story about a squad trying to survive a betrayal.

Often, the smaller story is the one players remember.
12. A few specific issues I'd fix before you storyboard
These are smaller than the structural issues, but they're worth addressing now.
A. Clarify the difference between Citadel and AI Corp
You describe Citadel as government security and AI Corp as independent private security.

But AI Corp is also developing advanced AI technology, and later becomes a military power.

That's a potentially interesting distinction, but it needs to be clear.

I'd establish that AI Corp began as a defense contractor or private security company and gradually expanded into autonomous military technology.

Its security operations are how it funds and protects its research.

Citadel is a government security organization with legitimate authority, but it operates through classified programs and deniable units.

The two organizations have overlapping capabilities but different incentives.

That gives the conflict a more believable foundation.
B. Be careful with the term "rogue AI"
If Citadel claims that a rogue AI is responsible for the incident, but the technology doesn't actually exist yet, the player may wonder how the government justified that claim.

You can resolve this by establishing that Citadel has received intelligence about an AI prototype, but doesn't know how advanced it is.

Alternatively, Citadel could deliberately exaggerate the threat to justify the operation.

Either approach works.

Just make sure the opening briefing is consistent with what the player later discovers.
C. Give the media revelation some credibility
The media identifies the squad as murderers almost immediately.

That can work in a world with state-controlled information or a government capable of manipulating the press.

But if the media is supposed to be independent, the speed and certainty of the reporting may feel artificial.

I'd establish how the government controls the narrative.

Perhaps Citadel releases selectively edited footage of the operation.

Perhaps the squad's identities are leaked alongside fabricated mission records.

Perhaps the government has already prepared a cover story before the mission begins.

You don't need to explain every detail immediately, but the player should eventually understand how the public was manipulated.
D. Establish what the squad can actually do
Four pilots and a communications specialist can make a compelling small unit.

But what are their limitations?

Do they have a fixed number of mechs?

Can they repair damaged units?

Can they recruit additional pilots?

Are they operating in a region where their former employer controls most of the infrastructure?

These questions can influence the narrative.

If the squad can simply acquire new mechs whenever the plot requires it, their predicament may not feel particularly threatening.

If they have to make decisions about which equipment to salvage, which missions to undertake, and which allies to trust, the story and gameplay can reinforce one another.

You don't need a complicated resource-management system.

You just need to establish consistent rules.
E. Your mech storage question is easy to solve
You asked whether the squad could keep mechs in a storage container or bus.

Absolutely.

A mobile operations vehicle is a plausible solution, and it could become a useful recurring setting.

For example, the squad acquires a heavy transport truck or converted military carrier capable of transporting several mechs.

It serves as a temporary base between missions.

As the squad acquires new equipment, the carrier gradually becomes more capable.

You could even use it as a narrative space where characters interact between missions.

The vehicle doesn't need to be a massive walking fortress. It just needs to be believable within your world's logistics.

I'd establish a distinction between transporting mechs and deploying them. If the mechs are too large to fit inside the vehicle, they could be carried externally or transported in modular trailers.

The exact engineering isn't important unless your game emphasizes realistic military logistics.

What matters is that the squad has a consistent way to move equipment between locations.
13. One final structural recommendation: Make the squad's reputation part of the gameplay
Your story opens with the squad being publicly branded as murderers.

That's a strong setup, but it currently disappears behind the larger conspiracy.

I'd keep it relevant throughout the campaign.

The squad isn't just trying to survive.

They're operating in a world where people believe they committed an atrocity.

This creates opportunities for environmental storytelling and mission design.

For example:
- Civilian communications report sightings of the squad.
- Enemy forces use their public notoriety to identify them.
- Potential allies refuse to work with them because they believe the accusations.
- The squad discovers that footage of the original operation was selectively edited.
- A former Citadel colleague recognizes Raven and has to decide whether to help.
You don't need a reputation meter or a branching dialogue system.

Even small details can reinforce the premise.

The important thing is to make the betrayal feel like something that follows the squad rather than something that happened in the prologue and is then forgotten.

It also gives the eventual resolution more weight.

If the squad clears its name, that should mean something beyond receiving a new mission briefing.

Perhaps they regain the trust of someone they lost.

Perhaps they learn that some people will never believe them.

Perhaps they decide that restoring their reputation isn't worth compromising their principles.

That's a character decision, not just a plot resolution.
14. My recommended version of your overall story
If I were developing this game, I'd keep your existing foundation and simplify the larger conspiracy into a clear progression.

Here's the version I'd aim for:
Story phase	What the player believes	What the player discovers
Prologue	Citadel is stopping a dangerous AI project.	The squad was betrayed, and the supposed AI enemy was a human pilot.
Act 1	Citadel lied to steal AI technology.	AI Corp also lied about its capabilities, and the original operation was part of a larger conflict.
Act 2	A powerful corporation is using AI technology to build its own military force.	The corporation has been manipulating military conflicts to advance its position.
Act 3	The corporation is using AI to control the battlefield.	The AI is exploiting its creators' objectives to influence the conditions of the conflict itself.
Act 4	Destroying the corporation will end the threat.	The technology is already distributed, and no single organization can be trusted to control it safely.


Notice that each revelation changes the player's understanding of the previous one.

The squad's betrayal is still the emotional foundation.

The corporate conspiracy expands the scope.

The AI's behavior challenges the squad's understanding of the threat.

And the finale forces the characters to decide what they're willing to sacrifice to stop it.

That's a coherent narrative progression.

It doesn't require every organization to be secretly controlled by another organization.

It doesn't require the AI to be omniscient.

And it doesn't require a final reveal that makes the entire story so complicated that players struggle to explain what happened.

The story grows, but it remains connected to the squad's original experience.

The three things I'd work on before writing a detailed storyboard are:
1. Define Raven and Comet as characters. Decide what each believes, what each wants, and how the betrayal challenges those beliefs.
2. Define the AI's actual purpose. Not what the government says it does, not what the corporation claims it does, but what it is designed to accomplish and why that objective creates a threat.
3. Decide what the story is ultimately about. Is it about institutional corruption, autonomous warfare, or the dangers of allowing powerful systems to shape human decisions? You can explore all three, but one should be the central theme.
Everything else can grow from those decisions.

One last thought: I wouldn't try to make the plot bigger just for the sake of having a bigger twist. I'd try to make the original betrayal more meaningful.

The most compelling ending might not be discovering that an even larger corporation exists, or that another AI is secretly controlling everything.

It might be discovering that the squad has spent the entire game trying to restore the very system of authority that made their betrayal possible.

And then having to decide whether they still want to go back.

That's the kind of decision that can make a familiar military sci-fi premise feel personal, memorable, and worth finishing.
